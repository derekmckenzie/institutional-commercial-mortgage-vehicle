# Accounting and Capital Treatment

## 1. What the archived materials actually document

The original working materials contain a specific accounting observation regarding **one proposed MIE owner**: the analysis suggested that the participant might hold power and a variable interest and therefore might be required to consolidate the MIE.

The materials also show that tax and regulatory questions were being actively examined.

That is the documented accounting / regulatory work.

## 2. What the archived materials do not establish

They do **not** provide a final accounting conclusion for the Canadian life insurer on:

- off-balance-sheet treatment;
- derecognition of a mortgage interest;
- consolidation of the MIE;
- continuing involvement; or
- regulatory-capital relief.

## 3. Project-author clarification

The project author has clarified that an intended benefit was for the MIE's share of the mortgage exposure to be separately owned and funded rather than retained on the insurer's own balance sheet, where commercial mortgages were capital-intensive.

That is a **design objective / commercial context**, not an accounting conclusion.

## 4. Analytical questions added for completeness

The following items are **not stated as resolved conclusions in the archived working materials**. They are the accounting / regulatory questions that would need to be tested if the intended balance-sheet outcome mattered to the economics:

- who legally owns each mortgage participation;
- whether the insurer retains control over the MIE or transferred interest;
- the effect of servicing and administration rights;
- recourse or other continuing involvement;
- recognition / derecognition of the transferred participation;
- consolidation of the MIE by any participant; and
- applicable regulatory-capital treatment.

These are **questions to be tested**, not conclusions drawn from the archived materials.

## 5. Why the distinction matters

A legal entity, an economic transfer and an accounting outcome are not automatically the same thing.

A rigorous transaction design would need the legal, accounting, tax, regulatory and economic analyses to agree before relying on any balance-sheet benefit.
